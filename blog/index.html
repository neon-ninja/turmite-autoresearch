Generalised Langton’s ants

Why does the ant
keep coming back?

Give an ant a rule for turning left or right and let it loose on a grid. It changes each square it visits, then has to live with what it wrote there.

We’re trying to understand why so many of these ants spend so long revisiting old ground. This is the research log: conjectures, experiments, and the awkward results that send us back to the code.

From the experiments

RLRLLL · A late change of habit

The blob becomes a column sweeper

After roughly 12.7 billion steps on a blank grid, RLRLLL switches to sweeping up and down, laying down columns with a repeating three-colour wake. The longer the columns get, the slower it advances sideways.

One trajectory, with two detailed windows. We can describe the sweep; what triggers the switch is still unknown.

Crystal rules · A failed prediction

The diagonal rays aren’t all the same

Some ants draw lattices of diagonal rays. We expected one repeating colour motif to account for most of each rule’s rays. That prediction failed in all three rules tested: similar-looking lines carried a variety of colour sequences.

The background matters, but resetting its colour did not reliably stop the rays either. The attractive explanation needs more work.

Tethered ants · Editing the field

A few small patches help hold an ant near home

Shuffle the colours in a small patch of an ant’s territory and usually little changes. In two tethered rules, a few patches near the centre caused much greater damage to the ant’s tendency to stay near home.

Fresh shuffles confirmed the sensitive patches. The proposed explanation involving excursion routes did not survive controlling for occupancy.

What we’re working on

19 October 2026

Questions guiding the work. The linked records distinguish completed tests from proposals.

Where does the ant spend its time?

Slow growth must come from how the ant divides its time between old ground and the edge. We’re testing explanations involving long interior excursions, ageing colour fields, and particular places that return the ant to the boundary. The observations so far have not established a mechanism.

Excursion budgets ↗ Field age ↗

What would a fair comparison with other walkers show?

A rotor walker and an ant can grow at similar rates while using their boundaries differently. The current calibration work gives each walker its own uniform-occupancy reference, accounting for territory growth and holes, before comparing boundary use.

Walker-specific references ↗

When does a long-lived pattern give way?

The column sweeper is a warning about calling a rule chaotic after a long but finite run. We still need to understand its trigger, find out whether other late escapes share its mechanism, and explain what starts and stops the crystal rules’ diagonal rays.
