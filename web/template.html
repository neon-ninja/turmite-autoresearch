Waiting for the highway

Langton's ant wanders chaotically for 9,976 steps, then suddenly builds a perfectly straight road and leaves forever. I ran all 4,083 rule strings up to length 12 to see how common that is. For six orders of magnitude, every tenfold increase in patience found about the same number of new highways. Then I predicted the seventh decade, ran a billion steps, and was wrong.

Each cell has a colour. The ant reads the colour under it, turns left or right as the rule says (R for colour 0, L for colour 1, and so on), bumps the cell to the next colour, and steps forward. Type a rule and watch.

Census

Two speeds of growth

Both clusters hold at every rule length from 3 to 12. The slow growers look genuinely logarithmic: their width rises by a few cells each time the step count doubles, so their fitted exponent keeps falling the longer you run them (it did for 95% of them). The chaotic blobs are the opposite: their exponent stays put near ⅓ from 65,000 steps to 134 million. I expected ½, which is what a blob would do if it filled new area in proportion to time. Why it is ⅓ instead gets its own section further down.

The main result

Patience pays at a constant rate

For each rule that eventually builds a highway, I measured the step at which the periodic motion begins. Grouped by order of magnitude, the new arrivals per decade barely change from a hundred steps out to a hundred million.

A constant count per decade means the onset times are spread evenly in log time: the density falls off as 1/t. If that held forever, the consequence would be unsettling. After a hundred million steps about 3,500 rules have no highway, and every further decade of computing would keep converting a steady trickle of them, with no point at which "chaotic" could be trusted.

Follow-up

Why a cube root

Almost every chaotic ant's width grows as the cube root of time. Before measuring anything, I wrote down a mechanism and the predictions it makes. Then I built an instrumented ant that tracks the visited region, its edge, and every step taken from the edge, and checked it against a brute-force recount on random rules.

Fresh ground pushes back
Every rule here turns right on a blank cell, so an ant that steps onto new ground curls straight back. The blank plane acts as a wall the ant can only chip at.
The ant is well mixed
Inside the visited region the ant's path is irregular enough that it spends roughly equal time on every cell. So its share of time on the edge is about (edge cells) ÷ (area).
The edge is compact
The region stays a solid, roughly round blob, so its edge has about √area cells.

new cells per step = c × κ × edge ÷ area ∝ 1 ÷ √area

Here c is the chance that a step from an edge cell breaks new ground, and κ is the ant's time on the edge relative to its fair share. If both stay constant, area grows as t2/3 and width as t1/3. The prediction is that c and κ don't drift with time. If the walker weren't well mixed, or the edge were fractal, the exponent would come out differently: ½ for a fractal edge, ¼ if the ant had to return home between growth spurts.

      How it was checked

      Method, and the trap I fell into

      The simulator is written in Rust in two versions: a deliberately plain one on a hash map, used as the oracle, and a fast flat-array one (about 10 ns per step) that the sweep uses. Property tests run random rules through both and compare every cell. The ant's rule is reversible, so another test runs it forwards and backwards and checks the board comes back blank. A single injected wrong turn at step 777 makes the suite fail.

      A highway is periodic motion with a net drift: for some period p, the ant's position after p steps has always moved by the same non-zero vector. My first detector accepted 20,000 steps of that as proof. It reported a cluster of highways starting about 22,000 steps before each million-step checkpoint, which is not how real onsets would be distributed. They were square-builders: the ant walks the edge of a growing square, and each edge is a perfect imitation of a highway until it turns the corner.

      The fix was to demand proof. A highway is now only counted when the ant keeps its periodic march all the way off the board and leaves in the direction it is travelling. Re-running everything that way removed 94 false highways, including the apparent record holder (period 36,416), and recovered 4 real ones that false alarms had cut short.

      That still was not enough. Some square-builders lay down each row in straight passes with period 4, and if a pass happens to cross the board's edge it looks exactly like a highway leaving. Five of those survived into the catalogue, all claiming to start three steps before leaving the board. The final check is that a real highway's onset belongs to the rule, not the board: every highway here starts at the identical step on two board sizes. That caught the five, and one more in the billion-step run. Each trap now has a regression test that fails if its guard is removed.
